Before any pipeline component could be built, four questions had to be answered: what our optimization scenario is, which models we could run, how routing quality would be measured, and where the labels to measure it against would come from. None of these were downstream implementation details. Each of them constrained the architecture that follows in \cref{sec:evaluation-pipeline}.

\subsection{Scenario: PropDesk}\label{sec:propdesk-scenario}

Interhyp could not give us access to their production chatbot or to the associated data. They provided the descriptions of their agents instead and suggested during our initial meetings that we replicate a structurally comparable setup in a different domain. The scenario therefore had a few conditions from Interhyp. It had to be transferable back to Interhyp's system, and it had to reproduce a comparable degree of router complexity and agent overlap. A further condition was our own. Since in the beginning we did not yet know how many tokens we could get for free or pay for, it also had to remain difficult even if the agent roster were later reduced from eight to five in order to lower running costs.

\subsection{Local Model Setup}\label{sec:local-models}

Neither the university nor Interhyp could provide a token budget, so any hosted capacity had to be paid for by the team itself. We learned this during Sprint 1, added SCRUM-22 mid-sprint to resolve it, and settled on running local models through Ollama, using Gemma 4 at whatever size each team member's machine could handle, with OpenRouter as the path for cloud deployments and a privately purchased token budget for the first tests of the optimizer, where the local model proved to be too slow.

The trade-off was that local models are weaker than production-grade hosted ones and results obtained on them do not automatically transfer, which is a threat to the external validity of everything we measured. What made the constraint tolerable is that our optimization target is a routing decision rather than a generated answer, and routing degrades more gracefully than generation when model capability drops, especially since Interhyp themselves use a smaller model for routing. It also turned out that the constraint had an upside we did not plan for. Ollama exposes token log probabilities, which is what Stage 2 of the router reads to produce a scored distribution over the eight agents instead of a single label (\cref{sec:router-stage2}), and that signal remained useful enough that the production configuration still routes Stage 2 through a local model even after AWS Bedrock became available for the judge and the optimizer.

Model capability was raised later where it actually mattered. Once the free AWS credits were confirmed in Sprint 3, the judge and the optimizer, the two components where a weak model directly corrupts the feedback signal, moved to hosted models, while the router and the agents stayed local. The reasoning is described in \cref{sec:judge,sec:optimizer}.

\subsection{KPIs and Metrics}\label{sec:kpis}

Following Interhyp's advice in the Sprint 1 review meeting, we fixed our KPIs early, as a router cannot be measured by the final answer's quality. For our pipeline, we established three primary metrics: routing accuracy (correct routes), agent precision (correct when selected), and agent recall (selected when required). We also proposed tracking router latency and token cost per route. While this was a general concern for Interhyp, it was especially critical for our local models, as even a slight drop in efficiency led to significant delays when multiplied across hundreds of test calls.

For Interhyp's post-implementation phase, we proposed metrics requiring live user data: first-touch resolution and cost per successful outcome. We also proposed clarification rate, the rate at which the system asks for more details, but deliberately excluded it from our pipeline, so that the system would not be incentivized to constantly request clarification.

\subsection{The Golden Dataset}\label{sec:golden-dataset}

\subsubsection{What Was Generated, and Why the Distinction Matters}\label{sec:dataset-scope}

Our synthetic data generation produces only the input side: a broker utterance paired with a categorical routing label, formally (prompt\_text, correct\_agent). No agent response is generated. This creates a single-label classification evaluation set.

Generation is LLM-driven and specification-conditioned, the standard setting, in which the instruction-following capability of the model allows controllability over the process and the production of datasets tailored to a specific application \autocite{long2024syntheticdata}.

Stage 1 aimed to mirror production: specification frequencies rather than a balanced set, difficulty spread across the full range rather than fixed at the hard end, because real broker traffic is mostly easy with a few awkward outliers mixed in. The intent was estimation, not diagnosis: an estimate of deployment behavior and, more usefully, a map of where the router fails rather than a single number for how often.

Once the router learns the easy majority in a realistic data mix, additional easy samples provide almost no new learning signal. High aggregate scores mask the fact that rare, critical boundary cases remain unoptimized due to their low frequency in the dataset. Stage 2 resolved this by generating exclusively high-difficulty items for all agents equally, ensuring we had enough ambiguous examples for less frequently called agents.

Stage 3 gave up on realism altogether and split all eight agents equally at maximum difficulty, giving every agent the same weight and the most ambitious prompts.

Stage 4 served as a 2,000-item sanity check to verify whether Stage 3's results generalized. It reintroduced a slight class skew and less complex prompts, while still including all of the complex ones.

\subsubsection{Difficulty and Label Integrity}\label{sec:dataset-difficulty}

Difficulty was engineered rather than sampled. Following the behavioral testing methodology of \textcite{ribeiro2020checklist}, every prompt was perturbed to attack one specific capability the router needs, through a fixed catalogue of nine mechanisms: a buried or ambiguous trigger, typographical errors, non-native grammar, an unusual synonym, a long multi-topic text hiding the real request, pasted-in irrelevant content, a keyword seeded from a competing agent, domain-term camouflage, and code-switching into German.

The constraint on the catalogue matters more than its contents. Every perturbation must be label-preserving: it may disguise the correct agent, but it may never change it. An item that a competent human applying the labeling rules cannot resolve is not a hard example, it is a specification defect, so a random sample was read manually and any batch containing such items was regenerated.

The remaining risk is contamination. As Interhyp pointed out in one of our mid-sprint alignment meetings, seeding the router's Stage 1 vector index from the evaluation set would let the embedding model \enquote{succeed} on queries it had already seen and make the accuracy figure unusable. Stage 1 examples were therefore drawn from a separate part of the dataset, or from an earlier generation of it that had been retired from evaluation use, so that no prompt was ever both a seed example and a live evaluation item.

\subsubsection{Limitations}\label{sec:dataset-limitations}

It is important to emphasize that synthetic data generation was never the primary deliverable of our task, and we deliberately spent less effort on it than on the router, the judge, and the optimizer. Our goal was never to perfectly replicate Interhyp's exact production metrics. Interhyp already has access to their own prompts, so attempting to synthetically recreate freely available data offers them no real value. Instead, we simply needed a functional dataset for pipeline optimization. Because these are straightforward text prompts, a manual read of a sample was enough to confirm that each prompt is something a broker could plausibly write and that its label is correct, which is what our optimization needed.

Since the generated prompts are deliberately harder than plausible production traffic, the accuracy figures from Stages 2 to 4 act as a diagnostic stress test rather than a production estimate, while only Stage 1 was built to approximate deployment behavior.
